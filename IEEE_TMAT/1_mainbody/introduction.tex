Ferroelectricity in doped HfO$_2$ and especially in \hzo{} has enabled scalable memory devices compatible with CMOS and BEOL integration~\cite{boscke2011,muller2012,schroeder2022}. For ferroelectric capacitors, however, the standard readout is destructive because recovering the stored state requires sensing the switched charge $2P_r$. A capacitive read avoids that penalty: the two remanent polarization states exhibit different small-signal permittivities, so a sub-coercive capacitance measurement can distinguish the stored bit without forcing a rewrite~\cite{luo2020}.

Recent work has shown that this capacitive memory window can be enlarged and even used for array-level non-destructive readout, but the reported values are usually quoted at a single frequency near room temperature~\cite{mukherjee2023iedm,lee2025aem}. That leaves the controlling physics unresolved. A finite zero-bias window cannot follow from polarization magnitude alone, because an ideally symmetric stack would still make the two $C$--$V$ branches intersect at zero bias. The missing question is therefore not whether polarization is present, but what sets the bandwidth, temperature range, and portability of the zero-bias read margin across device stacks.

Here we test two competing pictures. If the CMW is ferroelectric-limited, its dynamics should track the intrinsic dielectric and switching response of the \hzo{} layer. If it is interface-limited, its characteristic time should instead follow the interfacial impedance and change strongly with stack design and temperature. The reduced manuscript is organized around that question and the answer it supports: polarization stores the bit, but the interface sets whether and how fast the two states remain capacitively distinguishable.
